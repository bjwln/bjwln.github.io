LLM-Based Multi-Agent Systems for Oncology Multidisciplinary Tumor Boards: A Scoping Review
Version date: 2026-08-15
Search date: 2026-08-15
Reporting structure: PRISMA-ScR; protocol and registration intentionally omitted per request
Manuscript nature: final working draft for reading, revision, and future submission
Abstract
Background: Multidisciplinary team (MDT) meetings, commonly called tumor boards, are a core organizational format for oncology decision-making. They face growing pressure from rising case volumes, fragmented information, rapidly updating guidelines, and limited specialist time. Large language models (LLMs) have been applied to support MDT preparation, information extraction, and decision recommendations; more recently, researchers have organized multiple LLM agents into collaborative systems that attempt to reproduce the discussion, challenge, and consensus-building processes of a real MDT.
Objective: To map the current state of LLM-based multi-agent systems in oncology MDT and related clinical decision-making, and to answer four questions: which architectures are used; which clinical tasks are covered and to what degree; which public benchmarks and datasets are available; and which gaps and risks remain in the current evidence.
Methods: We searched PubMed, Europe PMC, OpenAlex, and arXiv through August 15, 2026. The initial search returned 328 records; after deduplication and topic screening, 108 new articles were included and combined with 136 existing records in the Zotero collection "智能体文献" as the evidence base. Because the included studies were heterogeneous in design, outcomes, and evaluation data, we used narrative synthesis organized around system architecture, clinical function, benchmarks, and risk.
Results: Included studies show four main architecture families: collaborative, debate-based, hierarchical, and hybrid/self-evolving. Clinical functions cover pre-meeting case summarization, structured information extraction, guideline retrieval and interpretation, diagnosis and staging, treatment recommendations, MDT consensus simulation, and early deployment pilots. Representative systems include MDAgents, MedAgents, MDTeamGPT, EvoMDT, RareAgents, D2MDT, and TumorBoard. Public benchmarks include MedAgentBench, AgentClinic, MedAgentBoard, MTBBench, LungCURE, PhysicianBench, and MedSentry. Most evidence remains retrospective, simulation-based, or reliant on expert evaluation; no included study supports autonomous clinical decision-making by these systems.
Conclusions: LLM-based multi-agent systems are a promising direction for MDT intelligence but remain assistive tools. Future research should prioritize real-world clinical validation, standardized reporting, interpretability, safety mechanisms, cross-center data protection, and long-term outcome evaluation.
Keywords: multi-agent systems; large language models; multidisciplinary tumor boards; artificial intelligence; clinical decision support; scoping review
1. Introduction
Oncology MDTs typically bring together medical oncologists, surgeons, radiation oncologists, radiologists, pathologists, nurses, and psychosocial staff, who produce structured diagnostic, staging, and treatment recommendations through structured discussion. Multidisciplinary deliberation reduces decision disagreement, improves guideline adherence, and improves treatment pathways for some tumor types (Gouliaev et al., 2025; Koo et al., 2025; Seif El Dahan et al., 2023). At the same time, MDT workflows face rising case volumes, more information dimensions introduced by precision medicine, and limited availability of senior specialists. Using artificial intelligence to help MDTs "do more, do it faster, and do it more traceably" has therefore become an important topic in oncology informatics.
Early studies evaluated single LLMs (e.g., ChatGPT, GPT-4o) against MDT decisions. They showed that LLMs can produce reasonable recommendations for some tumor types, but their accuracy is unstable and they lack an explicit multidisciplinary reasoning structure (Konzmann et al., 2026). The systematic review by Konzmann et al. synthesized the application, accuracy, and safety of LLMs in tumor boards, but its focus is largely the single LLM as a decision-support tool. In contrast, this review focuses on systems composed of multiple LLM agents and analyzes how such systems simulate specialty division of labor, evidence exchange, debate, and consensus formation. Broader reviews of AI-based decision support, precision oncology, and generative AI in clinical oncology provide the surrounding context for this transition (Goda et al., 2025; Oehring et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2026).
The specific research questions are:
RQ1: Which architectures do LLM-based multi-agent systems use in oncology MDT and related clinical decision-making?
RQ2: Which parts of the clinical workflow have been addressed, and to what degree?
RQ3: Which public benchmarks, datasets, and evaluation methods are available?
RQ4: What are the main limitations, risks, and research gaps in the current evidence?
2. Methods
2.1 Scope and inclusion criteria
Inclusion criteria:
Population/context: oncology MDT, molecular tumor board (MTB), or clinical scenarios closely related to multidisciplinary oncology decision-making;
Intervention: systems composed of multiple LLM agents, including role specialization, multi-round discussion, retrieval augmentation, tool use, consensus formation, or self-evolution;
Comparator: single LLM, conventional decision-support systems, or human MDT decisions;
Outcomes: system architecture, feasibility, accuracy, concordance, safety, clinical deployment status, or evaluation methods;
Document types: original research, preprints, conference papers, reviews, and benchmark papers;
Language: Chinese or English.
Records that used only a single LLM, discussed only generic multi-agent theory without medical or oncology application, or were clearly unrelated to the topic were excluded.
2.2 Information sources and search strategy
We searched PubMed, Europe PMC, OpenAlex, and arXiv up to August 15, 2026. Representative query combinations included:
tumor board OR multidisciplinary team OR molecular tumor board AND multi-agent OR agentic OR LLM agent
medical OR clinical OR oncology AND multi-agent LLM OR LLM-based multi-agent system
cancer OR oncology AND agentic AI OR AI agent OR autonomous agent
medical benchmark OR clinical benchmark AND agent OR multi-agent OR tumor board
The four sources returned 328 records in total (PubMed 4, Europe PMC 203, OpenAlex 21, arXiv 100). After removing duplicates and screening by title, abstract, and available metadata, 120 candidate records were retained. After removing items already indexed in the Zotero library and residual noise, 108 new articles were included and combined with 136 existing records in the Zotero "智能体文献" folder, yielding 244 unique evidence records in total.
2.3 Screening and data extraction
Screening proceeded in three stages: record deduplication, title and abstract screening, and metadata review and classification. For each included record we extracted authors, year, journal/platform, system name, architecture type, application scenario, evaluation task, main results, and risk description. Some conference abstracts or preprints were available as metadata only; they could still be classified by architecture but carry lower evidentiary weight.
2.4 Data extraction and synthesis
Because the included studies were highly heterogeneous in design, outcome definitions, and evaluation data, quantitative pooling was not appropriate. We therefore used narrative synthesis in four steps: first, establish a conceptual framework from single agents to multi-agent systems; second, classify system architectures; third, organize applications along the clinical workflow; and finally, synthesize benchmarks, safety, and risk.
3. Results
3.1 From single agents to multi-agent systems
An LLM agent is typically an LLM augmented with tool calling, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), memory, planning, and self-reflection, enabling multi-step tasks (Guo et al., 2024). Multi-agent systems go further by letting multiple role-specific LLM instances cooperate through division of labor, discussion, voting, or hierarchical orchestration (Li, X. et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2026).
The motivation in medicine is direct: a real MDT depends on complementary expertise, mutual questioning, and final consensus. Delegating these processes to a group of role-specific agents could in principle preserve specialty perspectives while improving reasoning transparency and evidence traceability (Tang et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2024). Several reviews have summarized medical multi-agent systems from the perspectives of workflow, infrastructure, evaluation, and safety (Abdollahi et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026; Xiong et al., 2026; Zhou, Y. et al., 2026; Lin et al., 2025; Guo, X. et al., 2026), but dedicated multi-agent reviews for oncology MDT remain rare.
The field is growing rapidly: 96 of the 108 newly included records (about 89%) were published in 2025-2026 (58 in 2026 and 38 in 2025); across the combined evidence base, approximately 79% of the 222 records with identifiable years were published in 2025-2026.
3.2 System architecture taxonomy
3.2.1 Collaborative architectures
Collaborative systems assign role-specific agents to process evidence relevant to their "specialty" and aggregate the outputs into a unified result. MedAgents is one of the earliest medical multi-agent frameworks, improving zero-shot medical reasoning through role-playing and collaborative discussion (Tang et al., 2024). MDAgents automatically chooses between a single-agent structure and team collaboration according to task difficulty, representing adaptive collaboration (Kim et al., 2024). TeamMedAgents formalizes structured teamwork for medical decision-making (Mishra et al., 2025), and ENTAgents applies agent teams to complex otolaryngology knowledge (Chan et al., 2025). MAGDA organizes guideline-driven diagnostic assistance (Bani-Harouni et al., 2024), mediator-guided collaboration coordinates open-source models (Chen, K. et al., 2025, Mediator-guided), and conversational multi-agent LLMs enhance diagnostic capability (Chen, X. et al., 2025). Zodiac applies multi-agent diagnostics in cardiology (Zhou, Y. et al., 2024), and similar role-based teams have been explored for sepsis management (Iapascurta et al., 2025).
The same idea has been extended to multimodal and specialty scenarios. MAM decomposes the diagnostic process into general practitioner, specialty team, imaging, assistant, and director roles, achieving clear gains on multimodal medical data (Zhou et al., 2025). KG4Diagnosis uses a general practitioner agent for triage and specialty agents for deeper diagnosis (Zuo et al., 2024). ColaCare combines structured EHR expert models with LLM physician agents, moderated by a MetaAgent (Wang et al., 2024). CARE-AD, MedAide, and MACD target Alzheimer's disease prediction, medical intent fusion, and clinical diagnosis, respectively, all using multi-role information fusion frameworks (Li, R. et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2024; Li, W. et al., 2025).
3.2.2 Debate-based architectures
Debate-based systems reduce confirmation bias by having agents challenge, rebut, or audit one another. Multi-agent debate has been shown to improve factuality and reasoning quality (Du et al., 2023) and to mitigate cognitive biases in clinical decision-making (Ke et al., 2024). In medicine, MedLA organizes reasoning with logic trees and has agents compare and correct logical structures (Ma et al., 2025). "Within the MDT room" situates agent discussion in a real MDT context and emphasizes team-evidence-grounded debate (Kuai et al., 2026). Medical AI Consensus applies a multi-agent review process to radiology report generation and evaluation (Elboardy et al., 2025).
To address false consensus and silent agreement, the Catfish Agent deliberately injects structured disagreement, forcing the team to re-examine evidence (Wang, Y. et al., 2025). MedAgentAudit audits multi-agent execution logs and finds that many agents do not actually re-check evidence but repeat their initial opinions (Zhu, Y. et al., 2025, MedAgentAudit). Together, these studies show that debate must move beyond multi-round conversation toward auditable evidence-checking mechanisms.
3.2.3 Hierarchical architectures
Hierarchical systems use a coordinator or "director" agent for planning, task assignment, and result aggregation, while specialty agents work within their own responsibilities. The adaptive collaboration structure of MDAgents can be viewed as hierarchical scheduling. The GI oncology multi-agent system simulates MDT workflows hierarchically and achieves improved expert ratings in multimodal gastrointestinal oncology decision-making (Zhang et al., 2025). HARMON-E uses hierarchical agentic reasoning to extract structured oncology data (Gupta et al., 2025). RareAgents organizes rare-disease diagnosis and treatment as an autonomous multidisciplinary team (Chen, X. et al., 2026).
3.2.4 Hybrid and self-evolving architectures
An increasing number of systems add memory banks, reflection loops, and self-evolution on top of collaboration, debate, and hierarchical orchestration. MDTeamGPT combines consensus aggregation, residual discussion, and knowledge-base accumulation so that the system can update with experience (Chen, K. et al., 2025, MDTeamGPT; Chen, K. et al., 2026). EvoMDT updates prompts, consensus weights, and retrieval scope from expert feedback and outcome signals, making it a direct representative of self-evolving oncology MDT systems (Liu, Q. et al., 2026). OncMDT combines dynamic orchestration, iterative retrieval, and reflective coordination to jointly optimize decision concordance, completeness, and citation accuracy for oncology MDT consultations (Zhou, H. et al., 2026). D2MDT adopts a residual deliberation mechanism that updates only unresolved consensus items, reducing redundant discussion and improving efficiency (Liang et al., 2026). UCAgents anchors multi-agent discussion in visual evidence (Feng et al., 2025), MMedAgent-RL optimizes multi-agent collaboration for multimodal medical reasoning (Xia et al., 2025), and mixed-vendor multi-agent teams have also been tested for clinical diagnosis (Yuan et al., 2026).
3.3 Clinical application stages
3.3.1 Pre-meeting case summarization and workflow preparation
MDT pre-meeting preparation is time-consuming and is the most feasible entry point for multi-agent systems. The Healthcare Agent Orchestrator (HAO) generates molecular tumor board patient summaries through a multi-agent workflow and introduces the TBFact evaluation framework based on "model-as-judge" (Blondeel et al., 2025; Roy et al., 2026). A conference report describes a multi-agent decision-support workflow for molecular tumor boards (Brodin et al., 2025), and an ESMO study reports on AI assistance integrated into tumor multidisciplinary teams (Geukes Foppen et al., 2026). A Stanford team developed, evaluated, and deployed a case summarization system in a multidisciplinary thoracic oncology conference, representing a rare real-world deployment case (Ellis-Caleo et al., 2026). VISTA Architect uses a knowledge graph to precompute patient timelines, achieving high accuracy on 15 key conference variables across 1,180 patients while reducing the cost of repeatedly processing raw records (Kiiskinen et al., 2026). A CHI study further examines AI-assisted tumor board case preparation from a human-AI collaboration perspective (Li, J. et al., 2026). A Findings of ACL study likewise virtualizes multidisciplinary reasoning for clinical intake (Chen, H. et al., 2026).
3.3.2 Structured information extraction and registry reporting
Multi-agent systems can extract staging, biomarkers, medications, and other structured information from pathology reports, imaging reports, and electronic health records. HARMON-E achieves high average F1 across more than 400,000 clinical notes and scan reports (Gupta et al., 2025). Agentic systems in radiology are also being reviewed as they move toward structured reporting and imaging-based workflows (Bluethgen et al., 2025). An open-source multi-agent LLM has also been applied to cancer registry information extraction, automatically generating registry fields from pathology and medical reports (Aal Abdulsalam et al., 2026). A separate multi-agent LLM framework has been explored for ICD coding from clinical notes (Li, R. et al., 2024). Although such functions do not directly produce treatment recommendations, they provide the data foundation for MDT decision support.
3.3.3 Guideline retrieval, interpretation, and evidence grounding
Oncology decisions depend on up-to-date guidelines. A tumor board-inspired multiagent AI system interprets oncology guidelines through a multi-agent structure (Wang, J. et al., 2026). A hepatocellular carcinoma study combines multi-agent LLMs with guideline retrieval and consensus deliberation for HCC clinical reasoning (Saenz et al., 2026). Self-evolving RAG systems such as SEMA-RAG emphasize agents jointly maintaining evidence grounding across retrieval, reasoning, and reflection (Huang et al., 2026).
3.3.4 Diagnosis, staging, and treatment recommendations
Multi-agent systems now span multiple tumor types. The GI oncology multi-agent system achieved an expert composite score of 4.60/5.00 in multimodal gastrointestinal oncology decision-making (Zhang et al., 2025). An agentic system for breast cancer treatment recommendations was evaluated on 72 real cases with 1,147 case-specific rubric scores; the best configuration achieved a global score of 0.594 but still exhibited omitted recommendations, incorrect rationales, outdated claims, and overconfidence (Almeida et al., 2026). GatorOnco is an agentic LLM for colorectal cancer treatment planning adapted with large-scale biomedical text (Lyu et al., 2026). OncoSphere AI, a virtual oncology collaborative tumor board, multidisciplinary LLM agent teams for gynecologic oncology, and a virtual tumor board for myelodysplastic syndromes have reported preliminary results as conference abstracts or preprints (Desai et al., 2026; Wang, J. et al., 2025; Kuerbanjiang et al., 2025; Swoboda et al., 2026). Specialty and disease-specific reports extend this landscape to cancer pain assessment and management (Le et al., 2023; Liu, H. et al., 2026), palliative care (Brondeel et al., 2023), chronic liver disease (Singh et al., 2025), diabetic retinopathy treatment planning (Shen et al., 2026), acute ischemic stroke (Yan et al., 2026), and COPD/asthma management (Mavigoz et al., 2026). Surveys of LLM applications in radiation oncology (Ding et al., 2026) and of multimodal AI in urologic precision oncology (Rajaee Rizi et al., 2026) indicate the breadth of the field, while a capability-boundary study cautions that frontier LLM collectives remain limited on guideline-conformant oncology decisions (Zhang, S. et al., 2026). TheraAgent adds an evidence-calibrated multi-agent framework for PET theranostics (Chen, Z. et al., 2026).
3.3.5 MDT consensus simulation and dynamic consultation
MDTeamGPT, EvoMDT, and Consensus Matrix directly simulate the consensus formation process of an MDT. Consensus Matrix uses seven role agents (oncology, radiology, nursing, psychology, patient representative, nutrition, and rehabilitation) to construct a quantifiable consensus matrix (Han et al., 2025; Ni et al., 2026). D2MDT constructs multidisciplinary consultation evidence from EHRs and performs residual deliberation on unresolved consensus items for clinical outcome prediction (Liang et al., 2026). TumorBoard provides evidence-grounded multi-agent decision support for longitudinal neuro-oncology (Liu, Y. et al., 2026, TumorBoard). AI-MDT integrates multi-agent support into a lung nodule/lung cancer consultation platform within real workflows (Liu, Y. et al., 2026, AI-MDT). An earlier agent-based modeling study also explored patient-centered clinical decision support in neuro-oncology (Rodriguez Almaraz et al., 2024).

3.5 Benchmarks and public datasets
Evaluation of multi-agent systems is shifting from static question answering to dynamic simulation of clinical tasks; early evaluations of LLMs as clinical agents already emphasized task-context dependence (Mehandru et al., 2024). Existing public benchmarks can be grouped into four categories.
Category 1: Virtual clinical environments. MedAgentBench provides a virtual EHR environment for evaluating the task-completion ability of medical LLM agents in realistic workflows (Jiang et al., 2025). AgentClinic builds a multimodal simulated clinical environment and evaluates agents in simulated patient-physician interactions (Schmidgall et al., 2024). AI Hospital simulates outpatient encounters with Chinese medical records and NPCs, and proposes the multi-view medical evaluation (MVME) framework (Fan et al., 2024). Agent Hospital further simulates a hospital ecosystem with evolvable medical agents and provides a long-horizon benchmark for agent collaboration (Li, J. et al., 2024). DynamiCare constructs a patient-level dynamic diagnosis and treatment dataset based on MIMIC-III (Shang et al., 2025).
Category 2: General medical task benchmarks. MedAgentBoard compares multi-agent collaboration with conventional methods across diverse medical tasks (Zhu, Y. et al., 2025, MedAgentBoard). MedicalAgentsBench compares internalized reasoning models with externalized agent frameworks on complex medical reasoning (Shao et al., 2026). PhysicianBench evaluates LLM agents in real-world EHR environments (Liu, R. et al., 2026).
Category 3: Oncology- and consultation-specific benchmarks. MTBBench simulates molecular tumor board-style decision-making with multimodal, longitudinal, and evidence-reconciliation oncology questions, and shows that current LLMs remain unreliable in temporal reasoning and modality integration (Vasilev et al., 2025). LungCURE is built on 1,000 real lung cancer cases and covers TNM staging, treatment recommendations, and end-to-end decision support (Hao et al., 2026).
Category 4: Safety and process-audit benchmarks. MedSentry contains 5,000 adversarial medical prompts covering 25 threat classes and 100 sub-topics, and analyzes the robustness of four multi-agent topologies (Chen, K. et al., 2025, MedSentry). MedAgentAudit summarizes ten types of collaboration failure from 3,600 execution logs and automatically audits 14,400 cases (Zhu, Y. et al., 2025, MedAgentAudit). Case-specific rubrics propose a rubric-based evaluation method and validate agreement between LLM scorers and clinicians (Shah et al., 2026).
Most of these benchmarks are publicly available and can serve as evidence of "public data availability": virtual EHR (MedAgentBench), simulated outpatient clinics (AgentClinic, AI Hospital), real lung cancer cases (LungCURE), multimodal oncology consultation (MTBBench), multi-task medical evaluation (MedAgentBoard, MedicalAgentsBench), and safety stress testing (MedSentry).

3.6 Risks and limitations
3.6.1 False consensus and silent agreement
Multi-agent systems can acquire a convincing appearance by "reaching consensus", but consensus does not equal sufficient evidence. MedAgentAudit found that 16.63% of cases contained observations unsupported by evidence, 98.42% of discussion turns repeated initial opinions instead of re-checking evidence, and 42.73% of turns did not activate specialty-specific reasoning (Zhu, Y. et al., 2025, MedAgentAudit). The Catfish Agent study further demonstrates that systems need active dissent injection to reduce premature convergence (Wang, Y. et al., 2025).
3.6.2 Context collapse, hallucination, and overconfidence
Multi-round discussion lengthens context; MDTeamGPT specifically studies the "context collapse" problem (Chen, K. et al., 2026). LLMs lack reliable metacognition and cannot reliably judge whether their own reasoning is trustworthy (Griot et al., 2025). The breast cancer agentic system study shows that tool use and autonomy do not always bring gains and can introduce new error sources (Almeida et al., 2026). MedGuards uses a multi-agent system for reliable medical error detection and correction (Ma, C. et al., 2026).
3.6.3 Safety and privacy
MedSentry shows that topologies with more open information sharing are more vulnerable to malicious agent pollution, while decentralized architectures are more resilient due to redundancy and isolation (Chen, K. et al., 2025, MedSentry). MedAgentNet proposes a federated multi-agent architecture to protect clinical data privacy across departments (Mabrok et al., 2026). Medical data, multi-agent communication logs, and cross-institution deployment also create new governance and ethics issues (Barki et al., 2026). Multi-agent evaluation loops have also been proposed to improve the safety and trustworthiness of medical AI (Ghafoor et al., 2026). SafeRx-Agent further grounds medication recommendations in curated knowledge to reduce unsafe prescriptions and improve explainability (Wang, X. et al., 2026).
3.6.4 Insufficient evaluation and reporting
Most studies use self-built cases, expert scoring, or question-answering accuracy, with few unified outcome definitions and little prospective clinical validation. TRIPOD-LLM provides minimum reporting requirements for LLM studies but is still rarely applied (Gallifant et al., 2025). A benchmarking study of LLM-based agent systems shows that results depend strongly on the environment and task definitions across agent frameworks (Liu, Y. et al., 2026, benchmarking study). The optimization paradox study further suggests that local optimization in multi-agent systems may harm overall clinical performance (Bedi et al., 2025).
4. Discussion
4.1 Main findings
This review shows that LLM-based multi-agent systems have moved from "concept demonstrations" to "task-oriented applications": four mainstream architecture families (collaborative, debate-based, hierarchical, and self-evolving) have formed; functions cover MDT pre-meeting preparation, information extraction, guideline interpretation, diagnosis and treatment recommendations, and consensus simulation; and specialized benchmarks for virtual EHRs, multimodal oncology consultation, and safety have emerged.
However, "architectural diversity" does not imply "capability maturity". Most systems are still evaluated on retrospective cases, simulation environments, and expert ratings, and standardized studies that directly compare architectures are rare. The performance gains of multi-agent collaboration are significant in some tasks but insignificant or even harmful in others (Almeida et al., 2026; Zhu, Y. et al., 2025, MedAgentAudit). The current evidence therefore supports positioning these systems as "assistive decision-making and workflow optimization tools for MDT", not as replacements for the human conference.
4.2 Application maturity
By clinical translation maturity, current applications can be grouped into three levels:
Tool level (relatively mature): patient summarization, structured data extraction, and guideline retrieval. Representative systems include HAO, VISTA Architect, and HARMON-E. These tasks are auditable, have relatively low failure costs, and are closest to actual deployment.
Decision-support level (fastest growing): diagnostic hypotheses, staging, treatment recommendations, and consensus generation. Representative systems include the GI multi-agent system, MDTeamGPT, EvoMDT, and Consensus Matrix. Results still require clinician review.
Autonomous decision level (not yet feasible): no current evidence supports multi-agent systems making treatment decisions without human supervision. Even in deployment cases, systems work by "generating summaries for physician review" (Ellis-Caleo et al., 2026).
Most evidence still comes from retrospective cases, simulation environments, and expert evaluation; only a few cases explicitly report real deployment or workflow integration, including the Stanford thoracic tumor board summarization system (Ellis-Caleo et al., 2026), the AI-MDT lung nodule/lung cancer consultation platform (Liu, Y. et al., 2026, AI-MDT), and the ESMO-reported experience of integrating AI assistance into tumor MDTs (Geukes Foppen et al., 2026).
4.3 Relationship to existing oncology MDT reviews
The systematic review by Konzmann et al. mainly answers "how well do LLMs perform in tumor boards", including the accuracy, limitations, and safety of single LLMs (Konzmann et al., 2026). This review instead answers "how multi-agent systems work, what they can do, and how they are evaluated". The two are complementary: the former provides baseline evidence for single LLMs, while the latter provides a landscape of multi-agent architectures and task distributions.
For subsequent review writing, the architecture taxonomy in this review can directly serve as "methodological background"; the benchmark section can support a "public data availability" chapter; and the risk and discussion sections provide material for "research gaps and future directions".
4.4 Research gaps
The main gaps include:
No standardized cross-system, cross-task evaluation protocol, making accuracy figures across papers difficult to compare directly;
No prospective real-world MDT studies, especially on long-term effects on decision quality, clinician workload, and patient outcomes;
Little systematic research on "human-in-the-loop" interaction, including when clinicians should intervene and how to review outputs;
Limited integrated design of explainability and evidence provenance; most systems explain only at the output stage;
Insufficient multi-center, multimodal, and multilingual oncology data infrastructure;
Safety research focuses mainly on prompt attacks and malicious agents, with insufficient attention to data bias, calibration errors, and deployment drift;
Self-evolution mechanisms may introduce "less controllable with use" risks, but few studies evaluate reliability after long-term updates.
4.5 Future directions
Future research should prioritize four actions. First, adopt standardized reporting such as TRIPOD-LLM for the development, evaluation, and deployment of multi-agent systems (Gallifant et al., 2025). Second, build oncology MDT-specific benchmarks that upgrade resources such as MTBBench and LungCURE from "model evaluation" to "system evaluation", including human-AI collaboration and process quality metrics. Third, conduct small-scale prospective real-world studies, starting with low-risk tasks such as summarization and guideline interpretation to verify deployment feasibility. Fourth, design auditable consensus mechanisms so that every recommendation can be traced to specific evidence, specialty roles, and disagreement points (Han et al., 2025; Zhu, Y. et al., 2025, MedAgentAudit).
5. Conclusion
LLM-based multi-agent systems offer a new assistive decision-making paradigm for oncology MDT. Through role specialization, evidence exchange, debate, and consensus mechanisms, they partially reproduce the structure of multidisciplinary discussion, but their clinical value has not yet been confirmed by real-world evidence. These systems should currently be regarded as tools that enhance the MDT workflow, with priority deployment in low-risk, high-value tasks such as case summarization, information extraction, and evidence retrieval, while more rigorous benchmarks, standardized reporting, and prospective studies validate their effects on decision quality and patient outcomes.
